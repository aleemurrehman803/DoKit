Why your website images are too big (and how to fix it)

Oversized photos are the #1 reason pages load slowly. Here's a 5-minute routine to shrink them without losing quality.

9-minute read · October 2026

The problem in one number

A typical phone photo is 4000×3000 pixels and 3–5 MB. Most websites display it at 800–1200 pixels wide. That means you're forcing visitors to download 10–20× more data than they ever see.

Slow pages hurt everything: visitors bounce, search rankings drop, and mobile users on limited data plans just leave. Images are usually 50–70% of a page's total weight — so fixing them is the single highest-impact speed win you can get.

How to check your image sizes

Before fixing anything, find the offenders:

  1. 1. Look at the file size

    Any JPEG over ~300 KB or PNG over ~500 KB on a web page is suspicious. Hero images should ideally sit under 200 KB.

  2. 2. Compare dimensions to display size

    Right-click an image and check its natural size versus how big it renders. If a 4000px photo renders at 800px, it's 5× too large.

  3. 3. Check the format

    PNGs of photographs are almost always wasteful — JPEG or WebP will be 3–5× smaller at the same visual quality.

The 5-minute fix

  1. 1. Resize to the display size

    Use DoKit's Image Resizer to shrink photos to the width they'll actually display at (e.g. 1200px for a hero, 800px for article images, 400px for thumbnails). Lock the aspect ratio so nothing stretches.

  2. 2. Compress smartly

    Run the resized image through the Image Compressor. JPEG at 80% quality looks identical to 100% for photos but is roughly half the size. For graphics with transparency, keep PNG.

  3. 3. Consider WebP

    WebP is typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same quality, and every modern browser supports it. Convert with the Image Converter and serve WebP with a JPEG fallback if you want maximum compatibility.

Typical result: a 4 MB phone photo → 1200px wide, 80% JPEG → ~120 KB. That's a 97% reduction with no visible difference on screen.

Recommended sizes for the web

  • Hero / full-width banner: 1600–1920px wide, under 200 KB
  • Article / blog images: 800–1200px wide, under 150 KB
  • Thumbnails / cards: 400–600px wide, under 60 KB
  • Logos / icons: use SVG when possible — it's resolution-independent and tiny

Audit your whole site in 10 minutes

Fixing one image is nice. Fixing every image on your site is transformative. Here is a quick audit routine:

  1. 1. List your heaviest pages

    Open your most-visited pages (homepage, top blog posts, landing pages). In Chrome, press F12, go to the Network tab, reload, and sort by Size. Images will almost always dominate the top of the list.

  2. 2. Note the offenders

    Write down every image over 300 KB. For each one, note its displayed width (hover over it in the Elements panel to see rendered dimensions) versus its natural width. A 3000px image displayed at 600px is a 5× waste.

  3. 3. Prioritize by traffic

    Fix the homepage and top 5 pages first. A 2 MB hero on a page with 10,000 monthly visitors wastes 20 GB of transfer every month — and slows every single visit.

  4. 4. Batch-process the rest

    Once you know your target sizes, use DoKit's Image Resizer in batch mode: drop in dozens of images, apply one size to all, then compress the batch. An afternoon's work can cut a whole site's image weight by 80%.

WebP vs AVIF in 2026: which should you use?

JPEG is the old default. PNG is for graphics. But the modern web has two better options:

  • WebP — supported by every modern browser since 2020. Typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equal quality. Safe default for photographs today.
  • AVIF — 40–50% smaller than JPEG, noticeably better than WebP at low file sizes. Supported in Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge. The catch: encoding is slower, and very old browsers can't display it.

Practical advice: use WebP as your primary format in 2026 — universal support, big savings, fast encoding. Consider AVIF for hero images where every kilobyte matters, with a WebP fallback. Convert between formats anytime with DoKit's Image Converter.

Avoid PNG for photographs entirely. A 2 MB PNG photo typically becomes a 300 KB JPEG or a 200 KB WebP with no visible difference.

Lazy loading: the free speed boost

Even optimized images slow a page if the browser downloads all of them upfront. Lazy loading tells the browser to load images only when the visitor scrolls near them.

The good news: it's one HTML attribute. Add loading="lazy" to every image below the fold:

<img src="photo.webp" loading="lazy" alt="Description">

  • Keep loading="eager" (or no attribute) on the hero image — it should load immediately.
  • Always include width and height attributes so the page layout doesn't jump when images load.
  • Lazy loading plus compressed images is the combination that takes a page from 8 seconds to under 2.

Real before/after numbers

Here's what the routine above does to typical images:

  • Phone photo (hero): 4032×3024, 4.2 MB JPEG → 1920px wide, 80% quality WebP → 185 KB (96% smaller)
  • Blog illustration: 2400×1600, 1.8 MB PNG → 1200px wide, WebP → 95 KB (95% smaller)
  • Team headshot: 3000×3000, 2.5 MB JPEG → 600px wide, 80% JPEG → 72 KB (97% smaller)
  • Thumbnail grid (12 images): 12 × 800 KB → 12 × 45 KB = 540 KB total instead of 9.6 MB

On a typical article page with 6 images, that's the difference between a 12 MB page that takes 9 seconds on 4G and a 700 KB page that loads in under 2. Same photos. Same layout. Just sized and compressed properly.
