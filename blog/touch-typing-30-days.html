Touch typing in 30 days: a realistic plan

No gimmicks — a week-by-week practice routine built on TypeMaster's lessons and smart drills.

10-minute read · October 2026

Set honest expectations

Let's be real: you won't hit 80 WPM in a month if you're starting from hunt-and-peck. But 30 days of focused practice will get you typing without looking at the keyboard at 25–40 WPM — and that's life-changing for anyone who writes daily.

The plan below assumes 20–30 minutes of practice per day. Consistency beats marathons: 25 minutes daily outperforms a 3-hour weekend binge.

The 4-week plan

  1. Week 1 — Home row (days 1–7)

    Learn A S D F J K L ; and nothing else. Your fingers live here; every other key is a reach from home base. Practice in DoKit's typing lessons — accuracy first, speed later. Target: type the home row blindfolded (metaphorically).

  2. Week 2 — Top row (days 8–14)

    Add Q W E R T Y U I O P. This is the hardest week — your fingers will feel clumsy and you'll want to peek. Don't. Slow, correct practice builds the muscle memory; fast, sloppy practice builds bad habits.

  3. Week 3 — Bottom row + common words (days 15–21)

    Add Z X C V B N M, then drill the 100 most common English words (the, be, to, of, and…). These words make up half of everything you type — mastering them is a massive speed boost.

  4. Week 4 — Real text (days 22–30)

    Stop drilling letters; start typing real sentences and paragraphs. Use the typing tests to measure WPM and accuracy. Aim for 95%+ accuracy — speed follows accuracy automatically.

Five rules that make it work

  • Never look at the keyboard. Cover your hands with a towel if you must. Peeking resets your progress.
  • Accuracy over speed, always. If accuracy drops below 90%, slow down. Fast mistakes teach your fingers the wrong thing.
  • Keep fingers on home row. After every keystroke, return to base. This is the whole game.
  • Short sessions, every day. 25 focused minutes beats 2 distracted hours.
  • Track your problem keys. DoKit's smart practice drills target the keys you miss most — use them.

Seven mistakes that slow everyone down

Most people don't fail at touch typing because it's hard — they fail because of these fixable habits:

  1. 1. Practicing too fast

    Speed is a byproduct of accuracy, not a goal. If your accuracy is under 92%, you're going too fast. Slow down until errors nearly disappear, then let speed build naturally.

  2. 2. Looking "just this once"

    Every peek teaches your brain that the keyboard is the source of truth instead of your fingers. Cover your hands with a towel for the first two weeks if needed — it feels silly and works brilliantly.

  3. 3. Ignoring the pinkies

    Beginners underuse pinkies and overload index fingers. The pinkies handle A, ;, Q, P, and Shift — roughly 15% of keystrokes. Weak pinkies cap your speed permanently.

  4. 4. Skipping the boring drills

    Home-row drills feel pointless on day 9. They're not. Muscle memory is built by repetition, and there's no shortcut around it.

  5. 5. Practicing while tired

    Fatigue destroys form. A focused 20 minutes beats a sloppy 60. If your accuracy collapses, stop — you're rehearsing mistakes.

  6. 6. Wrong keyboard height

    Wrists should be straight, not bent up or down. If your wrists ache, your setup is wrong (see ergonomics below), not your technique.

  7. 7. Quitting in week 2

    Week 2 is the valley: you're slower than your old hunt-and-peck and it feels like regression. It's not — it's your brain rewiring. Everyone who pushes through week 2 makes it.

Ergonomics: set up once, benefit for years

Good typing on a bad setup still hurts. Five minutes of adjustment now prevents months of wrist pain:

  • Elbows at ~90°: your forearms should be roughly parallel to the floor. Adjust chair height first, then keyboard.
  • Wrists straight and floating: don't rest your wrists on the desk while typing — it compresses tendons. Rest palms only during pauses.
  • Screen at eye level: the top of the screen should be at or slightly below eye height, about an arm's length away. Looking down at a laptop for hours strains the neck.
  • Laptop users: prop the laptop up on a stand and use an external keyboard. This single change fixes the two most common posture problems at once.
  • Take micro-breaks: every 25 minutes, shake out your hands for 30 seconds. Typing is a repetitive-strain activity — treat it like one.

How to measure progress (beyond WPM)

Words per minute is the headline number, but it's a noisy one. Track these too:

  • Accuracy trend: the most important metric. It should climb toward 95%+ and stay there. If accuracy drops as speed rises, you're pushing too hard.
  • Problem keys: which keys cause most errors? DoKit's smart practice drills identify them automatically — typically it's the pinky keys (Q, P, Z) and reaches (B, Y).
  • Consistency: can you type 3 paragraphs without looking, or just 3 sentences? Endurance matters more than peak speed.
  • Real-world speed: test speed on unfamiliar text, not memorized passages. Your "real" WPM is usually 10–15 lower than your best drill score — and that's the number that counts.

Benchmarks: 25 WPM with 95% accuracy after 30 days is a solid result. 40 WPM puts you ahead of the average office worker. 60+ WPM is genuinely fast — most people never need more.

After day 30: the maintenance phase

Day 30 isn't the finish line — it's the point where practice becomes optional because daily life takes over. Here's how to keep improving without drills:

  • Type everything for real: emails, messages, documents — no more hunt-and-peck, ever. Real typing is the best practice there is.
  • One speed test per week: 3 minutes on a typing test keeps you honest. If speed plateaus for a month, do one focused week of drills on your weakest keys.
  • Learn the numbers row properly: most 30-day plans skip dedicated number practice. Spend a week on it — data entry speed matters more than people think.
  • Try the games: DoKit's typing games build speed under pressure, which drills don't. Fifteen minutes of games twice a week is enough.

Most people reach 50–60 WPM within 3–6 months of normal daily typing after the initial 30 days. The hard part — building the foundation — is already behind you.
