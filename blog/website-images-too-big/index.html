Why your website images are too big (and how to fix it)

9-minute read · October 2026

A typical phone photo is 4000×3000 pixels and 3–5 MB. Most websites display it at 800–1200 pixels wide. That means you're forcing visitors to download 10–20× more data than they ever see. Images are usually 50–70% of a page's total weight — fixing them is the single highest-impact speed win you can get.

How to check your image sizes

  1. Look at the file size. Any JPEG over ~300 KB or PNG over ~500 KB on a web page is suspicious. Hero images should ideally sit under 200 KB.
  2. Compare dimensions to display size. Right-click an image and check its natural size versus how big it renders. If a 4000px photo renders at 800px, it's 5× too large.
  3. Check the format. PNGs of photographs are almost always wasteful — JPEG or WebP will be 3–5× smaller at the same visual quality.

The 5-minute fix

  1. Resize to the display size. Shrink photos to the width they'll actually display at (1200px for a hero, 800px for article images, 400px for thumbnails). Lock the aspect ratio so nothing stretches.
  2. Compress smartly. JPEG at 80% quality looks identical to 100% for photos but is roughly half the size. For graphics with transparency, keep PNG.
  3. Consider WebP. WebP is typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same quality, and every modern browser supports it.

Typical result: a 4 MB phone photo → 1200px wide, 80% JPEG → ~120 KB. That's a 97% reduction with no visible difference on screen.

Recommended sizes for the web

  • Hero / full-width banner: 1600–1920px wide, under 200 KB
  • Article / blog images: 800–1200px wide, under 150 KB
  • Thumbnails / cards: 400–600px wide, under 60 KB
  • Logos / icons: use SVG when possible — resolution-independent and tiny

Audit your whole site in 10 minutes

  1. List your heaviest pages. In Chrome, press F12, go to the Network tab, reload, and sort by Size. Images will almost always dominate.
  2. Note every image over 300 KB, with displayed width versus natural width.
  3. Prioritize by traffic — fix the homepage and top 5 pages first.
  4. Batch-process the rest: one size for all, then compress the batch.

WebP vs AVIF in 2026: which should you use?

  • WebP — supported by every modern browser since 2020. Typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equal quality. Safe default for photographs today.
  • AVIF — 40–50% smaller than JPEG, better than WebP at low file sizes. Supported in Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge. The catch: encoding is slower, and very old browsers can't display it.

Practical advice: use WebP as your primary format in 2026, with a WebP fallback for AVIF experiments. Avoid PNG for photographs entirely — a 2 MB PNG photo typically becomes a 300 KB JPEG or a 200 KB WebP with no visible difference.

Lazy loading: the free speed boost

Add loading="lazy" to every image below the fold, so the browser loads images only when the visitor scrolls near them. Keep the hero image eager, and always include width and height attributes so the layout doesn't jump. Lazy loading plus compressed images is the combination that takes a page from 8 seconds to under 2.

Real before/after numbers

  • Phone photo (hero): 4032×3024, 4.2 MB JPEG → 1920px wide, 80% quality WebP → 185 KB (96% smaller)
  • Blog illustration: 2400×1600, 1.8 MB PNG → 1200px wide, WebP → 95 KB (95% smaller)
  • Team headshot: 3000×3000, 2.5 MB JPEG → 600px wide, 80% JPEG → 72 KB (97% smaller)
  • Thumbnail grid (12 images): 12 × 800 KB → 12 × 45 KB = 540 KB total instead of 9.6 MB

On a typical article page with 6 images, that's the difference between a 12 MB page that takes 9 seconds on 4G and a 700 KB page that loads in under 2. Same photos. Same layout. Just sized and compressed properly.
