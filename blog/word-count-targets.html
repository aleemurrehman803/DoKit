Word count targets that actually make sense

From tweets to theses — sensible length targets for common writing tasks, and how to hit them without padding.

8-minute read · October 2026

The myth: longer is better

You've probably heard "blog posts should be 2,000+ words for SEO" or "essays need at least 1,500 words." These numbers are cargo-cult advice. Search engines reward satisfying the reader's intent, not hitting a number. A 400-word answer that nails the question beats a 2,000-word ramble every time.

The right target is: as long as it needs to be, and not a word longer. That said, different formats have real conventions worth knowing.

Sensible targets by format

  • Social post (X / Threads): 40–280 characters. One idea, punchy.
  • LinkedIn / Facebook post: 100–300 words. Enough for a story, short enough to read on a phone.
  • Email (cold outreach): 50–150 words. Respect the inbox.
  • Email (newsletter): 300–800 words. One topic per issue.
  • Blog post (how-to): 800–1,500 words. Cover the steps completely; cut everything else.
  • Blog post (in-depth guide): 1,500–2,500 words — but only if the topic genuinely needs it.
  • School / college essay: whatever the assignment says. If no target is given, 750–1,000 words is a safe default for a short essay.
  • Product description: 50–150 words. Features in bullets, benefits in prose.
  • Cover letter: 200–350 words. Three paragraphs: who you are, why this role, why now.

Three rules that beat any target

  • One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph does two jobs, split it.
  • Read it aloud. Anything you stumble over while reading, rewrite.
  • Cut 10% after "done." Your first draft is always longer than it needs to be. The second pass is where the writing happens.

What Google actually measures (it's not word count)

Google has stated repeatedly that word count is not a ranking factor. What its systems actually evaluate:

  • Information gain: does the page say something the top results don't? A 600-word page with a genuinely new insight outranks a 3,000-word rewrite of the same advice.
  • Task completion: do visitors find what they came for and stop searching? High bounce-back-to-search signals thin content, regardless of length.
  • Topical coverage: does the page address the sub-topics a knowledgeable reader would expect? This is about completeness, not padding — five sharp paragraphs can cover a topic better than twenty rambling ones.
  • Experience signals: original photos, real data, first-hand testing. Google's guidelines explicitly reward content that demonstrates actual experience.

The takeaway: write until the reader's question is fully answered, then stop. If that's 500 words, publish 500 words. Padding to hit an arbitrary number actively hurts you — readers bounce, and Google notices.

More formats, more targets

Beyond the basics, here are targets for formats people ask about:

  • YouTube description: 150–300 words. Front-load keywords and links in the first 2 lines (that's all viewers see before "show more").
  • Meta description: 150–160 characters, not words. It's a pitch, not a summary.
  • Resume: 400–600 words across one page. Recruiters spend ~7 seconds on first scan — bullets, not paragraphs.
  • Academic abstract: 150–250 words. Background, method, result, conclusion — one sentence each, roughly.
  • Product landing page: 300–600 words of body copy plus headlines. Enough to answer objections; the design does the rest.
  • White paper: 2,000–5,000 words. The one format where length signals seriousness — but only if every section earns its place.
  • Video script (per minute): ~150 words of spoken English equals one minute. A 10-minute video needs roughly 1,500 words.
  • Podcast show notes: 200–400 words. Timestamps, links, one-paragraph summary.

Readability: the metric nobody talks about

Two pieces can have identical word counts and wildly different reading experiences. What actually determines whether people finish:

  • Sentence length: aim for an average of 15–20 words. Any sentence over 30 words should be split or simplified.
  • Paragraph length: on screens, 2–4 sentences per paragraph. Walls of text get skipped, not read.
  • Subheadings every 200–300 words: they let skimmers navigate and give readers' eyes resting points.
  • Reading level: most web content should target grade 8–10. Short words aren't dumbing down — they're respecting the reader's time.
